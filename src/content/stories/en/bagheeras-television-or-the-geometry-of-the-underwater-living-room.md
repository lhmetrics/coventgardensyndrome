## Bagheera's Television, or The Geometry of the Underwater Living Room

The cat Bagheera taught the household one strange trick — to look at their own Home from the outside, through the windowpane. The habit grew out of her own personal, unchanging ritual. Her Ladyship loved to perch on any available windowsill on the street side and gaze intently into the depths of the rooms. No invitation to come in ever held any power over her. She stayed outside, preferring to watch from beyond the glass what the two-legged staff were up to inside the Home.

At first, the humans worried; then they decided that for Bagheera, this was a kind of television. But at some point, a desire arose to see the familiar world through the cat's own eyes. That experiment became a firm habit. It turned out that the view "from outside" reveals the objects' true order and disorder more clearly, and lets you judge lines, volumes, and a room's hidden harmony quite differently.

This time, a genuine spatial fracture occurred. The joining of the worlds "inside" and "outside" produced an absolute, pure Looking-Glass moment — without the slightest involvement of artificial intelligence.

Looking down from the second-light window into the living room, the layered glass caught and superimposed two parallel realities on top of each other. Sunbeams projected the mosaic tile of the open rooftop terrace directly onto the ground-floor interior, creating an extraordinary optical phenomenon. The living room's space was instantly flooded with an illusory turquoise wave. The enormous brass lantern-chandelier now hovers in the depths of the murky water, the antique wall sconce glimmers somewhere at the very bottom, and the heavy oriental rugs have sunk beneath the solarium's ripples.

The camera captured what the human eye is usually trained to filter out automatically. Once again, the Home's architecture proved that familiar walls know how to change their geometry and live their own secret, deep life — the moment you simply shift your angle of view.
