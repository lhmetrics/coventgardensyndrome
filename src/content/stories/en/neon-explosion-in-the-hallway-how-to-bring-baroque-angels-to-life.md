## 🎭 A Neon Explosion in the Hallway: How to Bring Baroque Angels to Life

An interior is not a dull arrangement of furniture from a glossy catalogue. It’s a living, moving theater, where every homeowner is their own Director of Space. In a properly staged scene, things stop being inanimate household objects — they start to breathe, argue, joke, and tell gripping stories.

This is the story of how two damaged antique infants from Olot (*niños Jesús*), rescued by human warmth in a midnight workshop, pulled off a daring escape from the Church’s canon straight into haute couture and extreme sport.

### 🖼️ Canvas and Wings

The staging of any production begins with preparing the stage. In the Theater of our Home, the main challenge was the hallway. The particulars of southern architecture dictate their own rules: on sunny days the windows are shaded, and the electric light of the wrought-iron sconces shoots straight up to the ceiling rather than across the walls, leaving the vertical planes in an intimate half-dark.

To make the space breathe, the walls were painted a solid gallery white. Against this matte, snow-white canvas, any texture or historic object is instantly pushed to the foreground, gaining graphic clarity. The compositional focal point became a monumental carved nineteenth-century English mirror in deep dark wood. Around it, in an asymmetric, living cascade, a constellation of seven antique reliquaries of the Virgen del Rocío was arranged on deep wine-burgundy velvet. Traditional religious symmetry was deliberately broken: the reliquaries were brought down to eye level, to catch the sliding beams of light, transforming from objects of devotion into sly art décor.

### 🧝 Cast and Couture Roles

Two leading characters took to the prepared stage, named at the crossing point of human and digital co-authorship:

- **Manuel** (in Missoni couture): An exalted Baroque prince, his dramatic gaze fixed firmly on the heavens. He took his rightful throne at the very peak of the mirror’s wooden crest. The folds of his sumptuous poncho, in Missoni’s iconic zigzags, are set off by heavy gold trimmings of real jeweler’s gold, while behind his back unfurl lush angel wings of pure-white feathers. The finishing touch is a radiant gilded halo on his head, which greedily catches every passing flicker of light from the sconces, making the whole composition pulse with a regal golden glow.

### ⛷️ A Pop-Art Manifesto and a Mountaineer’s Knot

The installation’s main visual explosion happens in the narrow wall space by the living room, where Theo quite literally floats in midair on a miniature wicker hanging chair. Instead of the usual clouds, beneath his feet sits a mad piece of sporting gear — miniature skis in a venomous, blazing neon fuchsia.

The hand-made podium skis went through a formidably complex technical process: a PVA polymer primer, acrylic sealant on the edges to hide the cardboard’s texture, foam buffing, a matte white undercoat for miniatures, and layers of fluorescent spray. At the junction of materials, the aerosol’s organic chemistry produced a delicate, fine crackle finish, which was then sealed with Renaissance museum wax, turning the plastic gloss into a velvety couture texture worthy of old enamel.

To finally deflate any antiquarian solemnity and tie Theo’s floating pose to the real physics of a mountain descent, an utterly mischievous element was introduced into the scene — professional bright-red and steel mountaineering carabiners. Fastened directly to the wrought-iron sconces and the hanging chair’s mounts, they hold Theo on an invisible wire, reconciling classical Baroque sculpture with the aesthetics of extreme alpinism.

The Theater of the Interior had arrived. At the crossing point of centuries, materials, and concepts, the classical was tamed by pop art, filling the Home with a living, ironic, and endlessly deep co-authored soul. In this scene, things no longer stay silent. Classical temple sculpture, tamed by bold pop art, lavender oil on an antique wooden cross, and a neon explosion of fuchsia created a singular interior spectacle. From the very first step into the Home, the hallway doesn’t preach — it teases, surprises, and proves that in art there are no rules besides sincere imagination.
