## The Silver Giant: The Rescue and Upcycling of a Nine-Branch Girandole

This story began with a cup of tea. Or rather, with an antique porcelain cup and saucer that I'd gone to collect from an 85-year-old English lady. *"Take him too,"* she suddenly offered, pointing to a huge, dark silhouette in the corner of her sitting room. *"He's too big, too much wax, too many memories. He was my mother's."*

And so a new hero entered our Home – a floor-standing giant 165 centimeters tall. Black with a century of patina, with completely burnt-out, dead and dangerous wiring inside. But most importantly, he had **nine branches**.

### Candelabrum, Floor Lamp or Girandole? A Short Art-History Primer

In the antiques world this kind of piece is called a **floor girandole** (*girandole*) or a **candelabrum floor lamp**.

* **A girandole** is always a grand, monumental light with cascading, crystal or openwork branching arms that recall the jets of a fountain (from the Italian *girandola* – a Catherine wheel).
* **Purpose:** Giants like this were made in Europe (mainly in France and England) in the late historicist era (1880–1910) to light the formal spaces of grand houses: ballrooms, the hall at the foot of the main staircase, or theater boxes.

**Nine branches are an exceptional rarity.** The standard for mass-produced factory runs was 3, 5 or at most 7 candles. Nine-branch designs required the most complex calculations: the base metal had to bear the weight of nine candles without bending over time.

Our giant has no hallmarks – which is normal for large interior pieces from the turn of the century (makers' marks were often left on the felt undersides of the base, which haven't survived to this day). But his noble French or English origin is given away by the decoration: lush neoclassical relief of curling acanthus leaves on the arms and collars, and classic Victorian gadrooning ("spoons") on the elements of the stem. The body is cast in **spelter** (*régule*) and finished with high-quality **silver electroplating**.

### The Great Wash: A Secret Diving Suit of Old Varnish

The first stage of the rescue was like a surgical operation. The candelabrum was taken completely apart, and it turned out that all the silver plating was covered in a thick, sticky, monolithic oily film.

A deposit like this is a fascinating historical marker. In the nineteenth century, interiors were smoked around the clock by gas jets, fireplaces and oil lamps. A century of soot mixed with kitchen fumes and settled on the metal, turning into an amber, varnish-like film of oil. On top of that, in old houses candelabra were often "preserved" the old-fashioned way, rubbed with petroleum jelly or gun oil before being sent off to the storeroom.

We washed him in hot water, literally scraping off this century-old coating. And then magic happened: it turned out that this awful, sticky grime was exactly what had saved the silver plating from deep corrosion and decay. It had worked like a time capsule.

---

### The Drama of Restoration: The "Aladdin" Mistake and the Right Protocol

Our attempt to protect the cleaned metal with professional *Renaissance Wax* museum wax at first turned into a disaster – before our eyes, the lamp clouded over with a dirty grey veil. We had to carry out a real chemical investigation.

* **The chemical trap:** The popular cotton silver-polishing wadding (*Sidol Aladdin*) leaves an invisible protective layer of paraffins and mineral oils on the metal. Sealed by the wax into the micropores of the spelter, these foreign chemicals reacted under the wax armor, creating a "greenhouse effect."

Because museum wax is a completely reversible material, the metal could be saved without harsh rubbing. The layer of "chemical salad" was washed off entirely with solvent (*disolvente*) and degreased again with surgical spirit, which drew all the foreign chemicals and moisture out of the pores of the metal.

**The new, now flawless conservation protocol:**

1. **Cleaning with foam:** Natural white clay (*Piedra Blanca*), mixed with a drop of washing-up gel, made a gliding cushion that gently lifted the new blackening without a single scratch.
2. **Rinsing and degreasing:** Thorough removal of the clay residue with distilled water and a final wipe with surgical spirit. The metal was left chemically completely bare.
3. **The hot-wax method:** Applying *Renaissance Wax* and then gently warming the metal with a hair dryer. The wax melted instantly, turned fluid and was drawn into the micropores of the spelter by capillary action.

The excess was buffed away with microfiber, and the girandole at last gained its perfect, deep, transparent "museum" luster.

### Redesign: The Verdict on Glass and the Water-Pipe Camouflage

The Interior Theater is not just about aesthetics but also about the laws of physics. The original plan was to give the lamp back its glass shades, but trying on the heavy glass cups delivered a technical verdict: nine glass bowls at a height of 165 cm shifted the center of gravity upward, turning the candelabrum into an unstable, toppling pendulum. An attempt to cover the lamp with one big shared fabric shade failed too: it turned the elegant silver tree into a hulking, dreary outdoor mushroom heater, hiding the beauty of the casting completely.

The lamp called for "bare" historical candle bulbs. But how could one put up with modern white threaded plastic lamp holders that screamed of their hardware-store origins?

Ready-made plastic camouflage bought online looked flat and cheap. And so a solution was found where DIY pragmatism meets jeweler-grade model-making:

* **The base:** An ordinary plastic water pipe was cut into nine sleeves that sat snugly over the lamp holders, hiding the threads all the way down to the cup of each branch.
* **The anatomy of wax:** Using a glue gun, random, sculptural drips of hot glue were built up on the plastic tubes, imitating the living drips of a guttering candle.
* **The magic of Citadel:** The painting was done with professional *Citadel* model acrylics. A base coat in a dense, warm ivory (*Ushabti Bone*) erased all memory of the pipe. A liquid shade (*Seraphim Sepia*) ran by itself into the folds of the glue drips, creating deep three-dimensional volume and the illusion of dust gathered over the centuries – an exact match for our old wall sconces. A final drybrush highlight and a matte varnish took away the excess gloss.

### A Chronicle of the Mise-en-Scène: Before and After

| Stage | Visual image | Atmosphere in the interior |
| ----- | ----- | ----- |
| **BEFORE** | Black, sticky, century-old patina. Modern round frosted globe bulbs. A huge white conical shade completely hiding the cascade of branches. | The piece looked bulky, out of place – a "heavy" "outdoor mushroom" falling apart. |
| **AFTER** | A deep, transparent silver gleam with a noble dark patina in the recesses. In place of the lamp holders – flawless matte sleeves of "antique wax." | The lamp straightened its back. The cascade of nine branches reads flawlessly, severe and aristocratic. |

The final chord was a change of bulbs. The temporary globes gave way to matching frosted LED **"candle in the wind" bulbs with a warm amber glow (2700 Kelvin)**.

When these nine living, curving wicks light up at midnight, real interior magic happens. The amber light gently floods the living room, picking out the texture of the Chesterfield leather, the warm wooden ceiling beams and the patterns of the Oriental rug. Out of a water pipe, hot glue and a couple of pots of model paint came a complete, museum-grade illusion. The Silver Giant has come home.
