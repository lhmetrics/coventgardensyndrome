## A Secret Concert for the Lady of the Night, or What the Toys Sing About

When dusk thickens over the coastline, and a cool sea breeze drifts in through the Home's open windows, the dense air fills with the intoxicating, heady scent of Dama de noche — night-blooming jasmine. The hosts dim the bright lights, light candles, pour champagne into glasses, and carry on unhurried conversations to the quiet murmur of the waves. In these moments, it feels as though the antique late-19th-century Canadian Bell organ by the stairs begins to breathe, ever so faintly, with its vacuum heart.

But the real magic begins later — deep in the night, once the humans have fallen asleep.

At this hour, the residents of the organ's top shelf — the wise, vintage Dog, the good-natured Bear, and the eccentric Dolce the Monkey (the very one who, by day, loves trying on Pavlovo Posad mittens) — silently open their eyes. They hold their own private musical salon. At night, they don't need a human to draw sound from the ivory keys. The Bell instrument, which remembers hundreds of lives, knows how to play itself, responding to the touch of invisible hands.

—

The first movement of the night concert always belongs to the Monkey. She demands drama, storms, and maritime expression. At her silent command, the harmonium's bellows begin pumping air with the brutal power of a pirate frigate. The organ blares, roars through its registers, and shakes the walls of the Home with a majestic and menacing theme. In such moments, the ghosts of the Caribbean Sea, of storms and eternal wanderers, come alive in the darkness of the salon:

But when the candle burns low, and the Monkey, worn out from her pirate battles, falls quiet at the edge of the wooden music stand, the character of the night salon changes completely. It becomes the Bear's and the Dog's time — a time for the Home's quiet, aching, deep confession. The Bell's bellows shift into a barely perceptible, trembling vacuum sigh.

The Vox Humana stop begins to sing the hypnotic, pure, cyclical melody of Michael Nyman's "The Heart Asks Pleasure First." The sounds fly through the darkness like drops of night rain, mingling with the thick scent of jasmine outside the window. This is music about everything the centuries-old walls stay silent about — about childhood dreams, about a young curate's youthful hopes, about the warmth people leave on these keys by day. The toys listen to this concert huddled together, and absolute peace spreads through their plush hearts:

By morning, when the first rays of sun touch the golden "Guelph, Ontario" mark, the Bell musical fortress will tightly close its mouse-proof pedals. The toys will freeze back into their usual, proper poses, pretending to be ordinary plush extras. And only the faintest trace of Dama de noche and a slight vibration in the old oak will remind anyone that the night in this Home has its own great, secret maestro.
